Listen to the forest. Langurs, chital, sambar and peafowl call when a predator is near — their alarms appear on the compass in red.

The forest

Pench Tiger Reserve, in the Seoni and Chhindwara districts of Madhya Pradesh, is the country of Kipling’s Mowgli stories. The Pench river runs north to south through dry deciduous teak forest and ends in the Totladoh reservoir.

The world here is generated from that ecology: teak, saja (crocodile bark), mahua, tendu, palash, the white kulu “ghost tree” on rocky ground, bamboo brakes and banyans; tigers, leopards, dholes, sloth bears, gaur, sambar, chital, wild boar, grey langurs, peafowl and mugger crocodiles; termite mounds, giant rock-bee combs, fireflies after the rains.

The seasons are central India’s: foggy winters, summers of 45 °C when the teak goes bare, the monsoon that floods every stream, and the green after the rains.

Music. No synth pads. The score is built from models of real instruments — a bamboo bansuri, a tanpura with its buzzing jawari bridge, the three-string bana fiddle the Pardhan bards use to sing the Gond epics, and the Baiga mandar drum. Exploration music follows the raga time cycle (Bhairav at dawn, Yaman at dusk, Malkauns past midnight, Malhar in the monsoon).
